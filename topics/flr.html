Key points

  • Treatment at relapse depends on how soon the disease returned (POD24), what the patient received before, whether the disease is refractory to anti-CD20 antibodies, and whether transformation has been excluded by biopsy.
  • Lenalidomide-rituximab (AUGMENT) became the second-line backbone, and adding tafasitamab (inMIND) or epcoritamab (EPCORE FL-1, HR 0.21) improved it further.
  • All four PI3K inhibitors received accelerated approval for FL and all lost their US FL indications between 2021 and 2023, a lesson in the limits of single-arm response rates.
  • CD19 CAR-T and CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies give complete responses in most patients after two or more lines, and are moving earlier.
  • Tazemetostat and zanubrutinib-obinutuzumab offer non-cellular, non-bispecific options in later lines.

Many options, chosen by context

Most patients with follicular lymphoma relapse at some point, often several times over many years, so relapse management is a sequence of choices rather than one decision. The main questions are how quickly the disease returned (progression within 2 years of first-line chemoimmunotherapy, POD24, predicts poor survival), what treatment came before, whether the disease is refractory to anti-CD20 antibodies, and whether a biopsy has excluded transformation to aggressive lymphoma.

Antibody-based therapy and the lenalidomide-rituximab backbone

Radioimmunotherapy was the first new modality: ibritumomab produced more responses than rituximab (80% vs 56%) without a difference in time to progression, and logistics limited its use1. EORTC 20981 showed that R-CHOP beat CHOP at relapse and that rituximab maintenance afterwards more than tripled median PFS (51.5 vs 14.9 months)2. For rituximab-refractory disease, obinutuzumab with bendamustine followed by obinutuzumab maintenance reduced the risk of progression by 45% compared with bendamustine alone3.

AUGMENT then established lenalidomide-rituximab: median PFS 39.4 vs 14.1 months with rituximab alone (HR 0.46), at the cost of more neutropenia4. It became the backbone for the next generation of trials. Adding the anti-CD19 antibody tafasitamab improved median PFS from 13.9 to 22.4 months (HR 0.43)5, and adding the bispecific epcoritamab raised 16-month PFS from 40.2% to 85.5% (HR 0.21), with more grade 3 or higher adverse events (90% vs 68%)6. Both triplets were approved in 2025.

Targeted agents, and the PI3K lesson

Four PI3K inhibitors received accelerated approval on the basis of single-arm response rates of about 40–60% in heavily pretreated patients: idelalisib7, copanlisib8, duvelisib9 and umbralisib10. Even a positive randomized trial, copanlisib with rituximab (median PFS 21.5 vs 13.8 months)11, did not save the class: toxicity, unmet confirmatory requirements and an overall survival signal in a related trial led to the loss of every US FL indication between 2021 and 2023. The episode is a reminder that response rates in single-arm trials do not guarantee benefit.

Two targeted options remain. The oral EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat produced responses in 69% of EZH2-mutant and 35% of wild-type patients, with good tolerability12, and zanubrutinib with obinutuzumab nearly tripled median PFS over obinutuzumab alone (28.0 vs 10.4 months) in a randomized phase 2 trial13.

CAR-T and bispecific antibodies

CD19 CAR-T produced the highest response rates seen in relapsed FL: complete responses in 74% with axicabtagene ciloleucel14, 69.1% with tisagenlecleucel, without grade 3 or higher cytokine release syndrome15, and 94% in third-line or later patients with lisocabtagene maraleucel, which also produced complete responses in 22 of 23 (96%) second-line patients with high-risk features such as POD2416.

CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies offer off-the-shelf, largely outpatient alternatives. Fixed-duration mosunetuzumab gave complete responses in 60%, and its 3-year update reported a median PFS of 24.0 months17; subcutaneous epcoritamab gave complete responses in 62.5% with little severe cytokine release syndrome18; and odronextamab gave complete responses in 73.4% and is approved in the EU but not in the US19.

Where we are now

Summarized at a high level (NCCN placement not checked against the current document, except where noted):

  • Late relapse, low tumor burden: observation or rituximab-based retreatment remain options.
  • Second line needing treatment: lenalidomide-rituximab, now often with tafasitamab (described as NCCN category 1 after anti-CD20 therapy in 2026 coverage)5 or epcoritamab6; obinutuzumab-bendamustine for rituximab-refractory patients who have not received bendamustine3.
  • POD24 or early relapse: biopsy to exclude transformation; consider earlier CAR-T, which is approved after two or more lines.
  • Third line and beyond: CD19 CAR-T, CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies, tazemetostat or zanubrutinib-obinutuzumab.

Open questions

  • Sequencing: CAR-T and bispecific antibodies have not been compared head to head, and the best order is unknown.
  • Which second-line triplet: tafasitamab and epcoritamab additions have not been compared.
  • Fixed duration and cure: whether deep responses to T-cell therapies are durable enough to change the course of the disease.
  • Infections and cost: both rise as more intensive immunotherapies move earlier.
  • Accelerated approval: how to weigh single-arm response rates after the PI3K withdrawals.

What is coming

Ongoing phase 3 trials (listed under Trials to watch at the end of this page) compare zanubrutinib with anti-CD20 therapy against lenalidomide-rituximab (MAHOGANY), mosunetuzumab-lenalidomide (CELESTIMO) and odronextamab-lenalidomide (OLYMPIA-5) against lenalidomide-rituximab, tazemetostat added to lenalidomide-rituximab (SYMPHONY-1), golcadomide-rituximab against investigator's choice (GOLSEEK-4), and axicabtagene ciloleucel (ZUMA-22) and tisagenlecleucel (LEDA) against standard care.
