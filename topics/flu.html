Landmark studies in untreated follicular lymphoma

Observation and low tumor burden strategies, early-stage radiotherapy, induction, maintenance and risk tools: design, verified results, context, open questions and reception.

Key points

  • For asymptomatic, low tumor burden disease, watch and wait remains standard: immediate chemotherapy did not improve survival, and rituximab alone delays the need for treatment without a proven survival gain.
  • For stage I–II disease, 24 Gy radiotherapy remains standard; adding systemic therapy (CVP, or R-CVP once rituximab became available) improved progression-free survival in TROG 99.03.
  • For advanced disease needing treatment, rituximab transformed induction; bendamustine-rituximab became the most widely used regimen, with obinutuzumab and lenalidomide-rituximab as alternatives.
  • Rituximab maintenance roughly doubles progression-free survival without improving overall survival (PRIMA), so its use is individualized.
  • Early progression within 2 years (POD24) and a positive end-of-induction PET identify the minority with poor outcomes.
  • Chemotherapy-free, bispecific antibody-based frontline therapy is being tested.

The goal: control with the least toxicity

Follicular lymphoma is usually indolent and incurable with standard therapy, yet most patients live for many years. Management has therefore aimed to control the disease when it causes problems while sparing patients unnecessary treatment, and the main decisions depend on stage, tumor burden and symptoms.

Low tumor burden and early-stage disease

The British National Lymphoma Investigation randomized asymptomatic patients with advanced low-grade lymphoma to immediate chlorambucil or observation and found no difference in overall survival (median 5.9 vs 6.7 years)1. Watch and wait remains standard. Rituximab then offered a low-toxicity alternative: extended scheduling prolonged remission2, and in a UK trial 88% of patients given rituximab with maintenance had not needed new treatment at 3 years, compared with 46% under observation, without a demonstrated survival difference3. RESORT showed that retreating at progression worked as well as scheduled maintenance, with far fewer doses (median 4 vs 18)4.

For stage I–II disease, radiotherapy is potentially curative; the FORT trial confirmed that 24 Gy is needed for durable local control, with 4 Gy reserved for palliation5. TROG 99.03 found that adding systemic therapy, driven by R-CVP, improved 10-year PFS (59% vs 41%) without a survival difference6.

Advanced disease: induction

Rituximab was the decisive advance. Added to CVP it reduced treatment failure dramatically (median time to treatment failure 27 vs 7 months)7, and added to CHOP it cut the risk of treatment failure by about 60% with fewer deaths8. FOLL05 favored R-CHOP over R-CVP and ended fludarabine-based induction9. Bendamustine-rituximab then became the most widely used regimen: in StiL NHL1 median PFS was 69.5 vs 31.2 months compared with R-CHOP across indolent histologies, with less toxicity10, and BRIGHT confirmed it, while long-term follow-up showed more second cancers11.

Two alternatives followed. Obinutuzumab instead of rituximab improved 3-year PFS (80.0% vs 73.3%) but with more toxicity, particularly with bendamustine12. Lenalidomide-rituximab gave the same long-term outcome as chemoimmunotherapy (10-year PFS 46.4% vs 46.6%), offering a chemotherapy-free option rather than a better one13.

Maintenance and consolidation

Radioimmunotherapy consolidation nearly tripled median PFS (36.5 vs 13.3 months) after induction given mostly without rituximab14, but its use faded once rituximab maintenance arrived. PRIMA showed that 2 years of rituximab maintenance after chemoimmunotherapy extended median PFS from 4.1 to 10.5 years, with no difference in overall survival (about 80% at 10 years in both groups)15, which is why many clinicians now individualize it.

Predicting who does poorly

The FLIPI16 and FLIPI-217 indices stratify patients at diagnosis, and adding mutations in seven genes refined risk in the m7-FLIPI18. Two response markers matter more in practice: a positive PET after induction predicts shorter PFS (HR 3.9) and survival (HR 6.7)19, and progression within 2 years of first-line R-CHOP, seen in 19% of patients, cut 5-year survival from 90% to 50%20. POD24 now prompts biopsy to exclude transformation and is an entry criterion for trials of earlier cellular therapy.

Toward chemotherapy-free first-line therapy

Bispecific antibodies, effective in relapsed disease, are moving into first line. In a phase 2 study, mosunetuzumab with response-adapted polatuzumab vedotin and obinutuzumab gave complete responses in 86% and a 2-year PFS of 89%, with only grade 1 cytokine release syndrome21.

Where we are now

Summarized at a high level (NCCN placement not checked against the current document):

  • Asymptomatic, low tumor burden, advanced stage: observation, or rituximab alone for patients who prefer treatment.
  • Stage I–II: involved-site radiotherapy (24 Gy), with systemic therapy added for selected patients.
  • Advanced disease needing treatment: bendamustine-rituximab is the most common choice; R-CHOP, obinutuzumab-based chemotherapy and lenalidomide-rituximab are alternatives; maintenance is optional.
  • After induction: end-of-treatment PET; patients with POD24 need a biopsy and referral for trial or early cellular therapy.

Open questions

  • Survival: neither early rituximab nor maintenance has improved overall survival; is earlier or longer treatment worthwhile?
  • Predicting POD24 at diagnosis: clinical and genetic indices identify risk only modestly.
  • Long-term harms: second cancers and infections after bendamustine and anti-CD20 maintenance.
  • Chemotherapy-free first line: whether bispecific antibody-based regimens can match or beat chemoimmunotherapy, and at what cost.
  • Cure: whether deeper responses can change the natural history.

What is coming

Ongoing phase 3 trials (listed under Trials to watch at the end of this page) compare epcoritamab-lenalidomide-rituximab with chemoimmunotherapy (EPCORE FL-2), mosunetuzumab-lenalidomide with chemoimmunotherapy in high-risk patients (MorningLyte), odronextamab alone or with chemotherapy against rituximab-chemotherapy (OLYMPIA-1, OLYMPIA-2), and surovatamig with rituximab against chemoimmunotherapy.
