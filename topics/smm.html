It is not a clinical practice guideline, medical advice or a substitute for clinical judgment. Patient care should follow the primary literature, current guidelines (such as NCCN, ESMO, IMWG and ASCO), approved prescribing information and the individual patient's circumstances.

Key points

  • Smoldering myeloma is a heterogeneous precursor state whose risk is front-loaded: about 10% per year progress in the first 5 years, much less afterwards.
  • The 2014 IMWG criteria moved the highest-risk patients (SLiM features) into active myeloma, so studies before and after 2014 describe different populations.
  • Simple risk models (Mayo 20/2/20, IMWG 2/20/20) now define high risk; dynamic models that track changes over time are emerging.
  • Early treatment of high-risk disease moved from trials into practice when subcutaneous daratumumab was approved in 2025 on the basis of AQUILA.
  • Guidelines still differ: NCCN lists lenalidomide as an option, whereas the 2026 ASCO-Ontario guideline does not routinely recommend it, and a Cochrane review rates the evidence for early intervention as low certainty.
  • Curative-intent and bispecific antibody approaches produce very deep responses but remain investigational.

A precursor state with uneven risk

The modern view of smoldering myeloma starts with the Mayo cohort of 276 patients: progression to myeloma or amyloidosis occurred at about 10% per year for the first 5 years, about 3% per year for the next 5 and about 1% per year thereafter, reaching 73% at 15 years1. Two biomarkers soon refined that picture: flow cytometry, where a high proportion of aberrant plasma cells with immunoparesis separated 5-year progression risks of 4%, 46% and 72%2, and the serum free light chain ratio3.

In 2014, the IMWG moved patients with any of three biomarkers of near-certain progression (60% or more clonal plasma cells, a light chain ratio of 100 or more, or more than one focal MRI lesion) into active myeloma4. That decision matters for interpreting every trial: patients called "smoldering" before 2014 included some who would now be treated as having myeloma. Population screening in Iceland later showed that smoldering myeloma is present in about 0.5% of people aged 40 or older, mostly with low-risk features5, a reminder that over-treatment is a real risk.

Defining high risk

Two simple models now dominate. The Mayo 20/2/20 model (marrow plasma cells over 20%, M-protein over 2 g/dL, light chain ratio over 20) separated median times to progression of 110, 68 and 29 months6, and the IMWG validated the same factors in nearly 2,000 patients, with 2-year progression of 6%, 18% and 44% for low, intermediate and high risk7. Because these models use a single time point, dynamic approaches that follow changes in M-protein, light chains, creatinine and hemoglobin are being developed; the PANGEA model outperformed the static models (C-statistic 0.79)8.

Early treatment: from feasibility to approval

Early attempts with thalidomide showed that treatment was feasible but poorly tolerated910. The field changed with QuiRedex, a small Spanish trial of 119 high-risk patients: lenalidomide-dexamethasone delayed progression and improved 3-year survival (94% vs 80%), with the survival benefit maintained at long-term follow-up11. Its definitions predated 2014, so some patients would now be considered to have myeloma. ECOG E3A06 confirmed that lenalidomide alone delays progression (3-year PFS 91% vs 66%), mainly in high-risk patients, without a survival difference12. Not every agent worked: IL-6 blockade with siltuximab did not meet its endpoint13.

Daratumumab provided the decisive trial. After CENTAURUS selected a prolonged dosing schedule14, AQUILA randomized 390 high-risk patients to subcutaneous daratumumab for up to 3 years or active monitoring: 5-year PFS was 63.1% vs 40.8% (HR 0.49) and 5-year overall survival 93.0% vs 86.9% (HR 0.52), with few discontinuations for toxicity15. Approval followed in the EU in July 2025 and in the US on November 6, 2025, the first treatment approved for smoldering myeloma.

Curative intent and immunotherapy

A parallel line of studies asks whether high-risk smoldering myeloma can be cured rather than delayed. Carfilzomib-lenalidomide-dexamethasone produced MRD-negative complete responses in 70%16; the transplant-based GEM-CESAR approach reached undetectable MRD in 62% after transplant, with 70-month overall survival of 92%17; and ixazomib-lenalidomide-dexamethasone linked MRD negativity to durable control18. Most recently, fixed-duration teclistamab produced complete responses in 77.8% and a 2-year PFS of 92% vs 49% with lenalidomide-dexamethasone in a small randomized phase 2 trial19. These remain trial approaches.

Where we are now

The first steps are to confirm that the patient truly has smoldering myeloma (no SLiM-CRAB features, with appropriate imaging) and to estimate risk. For low- and intermediate-risk disease, observation remains standard. For high-risk disease, guidance differs. NCCN (January 2026 version, as summarized on this page; not checked against the full document) recommends treatment with careful selection and shared decision-making, including daratumumab, and lists lenalidomide as an option. The 2026 ASCO-Ontario living guideline says daratumumab may be offered, does not routinely recommend lenalidomide and advises no therapy for non-high-risk disease20. A 2026 Cochrane review grades the evidence for early intervention as low certainty21, and the 2021 European consensus, written before AQUILA, favored observation outside trials22. In practice, the decision is individualized.

Open questions

  • Delay or cure: is the goal to postpone myeloma with low-toxicity therapy or to attempt eradication in the highest-risk patients?
  • Who is high risk: static versus dynamic models, and the role of cytogenetics and imaging.
  • Over-treatment: screening finds many low-risk patients who may never need therapy.
  • Later consequences: how early anti-CD38 or T-cell-directed therapy affects responses at progression, and infection risk in asymptomatic people.
  • Survival: only QuiRedex and AQUILA report survival gains, and both have limitations.

What is coming

Ongoing phase 3 trials (listed under Trials to watch at the end of this page) compare daratumumab-lenalidomide-dexamethasone with lenalidomide-dexamethasone with overall survival as the endpoint (DETER-SMM), isatuximab-lenalidomide-dexamethasone with lenalidomide-dexamethasone, and linvoseltamab with daratumumab (LINKER-SMM2).
