Patient care should follow the primary literature, current guidelines (such as NCCN, ESMO, IMWG and ASCO), approved prescribing information and the individual patient's circumstances.

Key points

  • Waldenström macroglobulinemia is lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma in the marrow with an IgM protein of any level. Treatment is for symptoms, not for the IgM number.
  • MYD88 L265P is present in about nine patients in ten, and CXCR4 mutations in about a quarter. Together they predict how fast and how deeply a BTK inhibitor works.
  • Two approaches are standard for first treatment: fixed-duration chemoimmunotherapy (DRC or bendamustine-rituximab) and a continuous covalent BTK inhibitor. They have not been compared in a reported randomized trial.
  • Zanubrutinib and ibrutinib were similarly effective in ASPEN; zanubrutinib caused less atrial fibrillation and is the usual choice among covalent BTK inhibitors.
  • Proteasome inhibitor regimens give fast responses, but bortezomib added to DRC did not significantly improve PFS.
  • After a covalent BTK inhibitor, venetoclax and pirtobrutinib are active; neither is approved for this disease, and complete responses remain rare with every drug.

Defining the disease and deciding when to treat

The 2002 workshop in Athens defined Waldenström macroglobulinemia as lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma in the bone marrow with an IgM monoclonal protein of any concentration, and separated symptomatic disease, which needs treatment, from asymptomatic disease1. Those criteria are still the entry criteria of trials. Among asymptomatic patients, 72% progressed over a median follow-up of 7.8 years, and IgM, marrow infiltration, β2-microglobulin and albumin separate groups with a median time to progression of 1.8, 4.8 and 9.3 years2. No trial has shown that treating before symptoms helps, and observation remains the standard3.

For patients who start treatment, the IPSSWM uses age, hemoglobin, platelets, β2-microglobulin and the size of the monoclonal protein; 5-year survival was 87%, 68% and 36% in its three groups4. The revised score, built in patients treated mostly with rituximab-based regimens, uses age, β2-microglobulin, albumin and LDH, with 10-year survival from 84% to 9% across five groups5. Both describe prognosis; neither selects treatment.

Genotype

Whole-genome sequencing found MYD88 L265P in 91% of patients with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma6, and then somatic CXCR4 mutations like those of WHIM syndrome. Patients with both mutations have more marrow disease and higher IgM; patients with neither have the lowest marrow burden, and wild-type MYD88 was associated with a higher risk of death7. The practical value of genotype came with BTK inhibitors, below.

Rituximab and chemoimmunotherapy

Rituximab alone gave an objective response in 27.5% of patients, and a companion series described the IgM flare, a rise in IgM in 10 of 11 patients that can cause hyperviscosity8. Combinations did better. DRC gave responses in 83% of untreated patients, with a 2-year PFS of 67% and little hematologic toxicity, although responses took a median of 4.1 months; in the final analysis, median PFS was 35 months and median overall survival 95 months9. Adding rituximab to CHOP raised the response rate from 67% to 94% in the only randomized test of rituximab with chemotherapy10. Bendamustine-rituximab then replaced R-CHOP across indolent lymphomas, with a median PFS of 69.5 months against 31.211; in the trial's small Waldenström subgroup of 41 patients, median PFS was 69.5 against 28.1 months. Two years of rituximab maintenance after bendamustine-rituximab did not significantly prolong PFS, 101 against 83 months, in a trial reported so far only as a meeting abstract12. WM1, the largest randomized trial in this disease, showed longer PFS with fludarabine than with chlorambucil, 36.3 months against 27.1, and more second malignancies with chlorambucil; neither arm included rituximab13. Autologous transplantation gave a 5-year PFS of 39.7% in a registry series and is now rarely used14.

Proteasome inhibitors

Bortezomib with dexamethasone and rituximab produced responses in 96% of untreated patients within a median of 1.4 months, but 61% stopped bortezomib early, mostly for neuropathy15. A weekly schedule gave a median PFS of 43 months with long follow-up16. Carfilzomib and ixazomib regimens avoided most neuropathy1718, and ixazomib with subcutaneous rituximab was active at relapse19. The randomized test came late: adding bortezomib to DRC shortened the time to first response from 5.5 to 3.0 months, but 24-month PFS was 80.6% against 72.8%, not a significant difference20.

BTK inhibitors

Ibrutinib gave responses in 90.5% of previously treated patients, and genotype mattered: major responses in 91.2% with MYD88 L265P and wild-type CXCR4, 61.9% when CXCR4 was also mutated, and 28.6% with wild-type MYD88. At five years, PFS was 70% and 38% in the first two groups21. It was also active in disease refractory to rituximab22 and as first treatment, where CXCR4 mutations again slowed responses23. In iNNOVATE, ibrutinib with rituximab gave a 30-month PFS of 82% against 28% with rituximab alone; the trial had no ibrutinib-alone arm, so what rituximab adds is not known24. A pooled analysis of three prospective studies found a longer 48-month PFS with ibrutinib-rituximab than with ibrutinib alone only in CXCR4-mutated disease, 72% against 43%25. Ibrutinib also improved symptoms in 85% of patients with Bing-Neel syndrome, central nervous system involvement26.

ASPEN compared zanubrutinib with ibrutinib. It missed its primary endpoint, the rate of very good partial response, 28% against 19%, and 18-month PFS was similar, 85% and 84%; zanubrutinib caused less atrial fibrillation, 7.9% against 23.5% in the final analysis, and more neutropenia27; its phase 1/2 trial had given a 3-year PFS of 80.5%28. Tirabrutinib, approved in Japan, and orelabrutinib gave major responses in 88.9% and 80.9% of patients2930. In patients without a MYD88 mutation, zanubrutinib gave major responses in 50%31. Acalabrutinib, not approved for this disease, has five-year data: a 66-month PFS of 52.0% in relapsed and 83.6% in untreated patients32.

All of these are continuous treatments with almost no complete responses. Progression on ibrutinib is associated with mutations at BTK Cys481, found in 3 of 6 progressing patients33.

After a covalent BTK inhibitor

Two years of venetoclax gave responses in 84% of previously treated patients, half of them after a BTK inhibitor, with a median PFS of 36 months in the long-term report and no effect of CXCR4 mutations34. Pirtobrutinib, a noncovalent BTK inhibitor, gave responses in 81.0% of patients previously treated with a covalent one35. Neither is approved for Waldenström macroglobulinemia.

Where we are now

The points below are a personal summary, not a guideline; NCCN placements were not checked against the current document.

  • Asymptomatic disease: observation, with follow-up set by risk.
  • Before treatment: MYD88 and, where available, CXCR4 testing3.
  • First treatment: fixed-duration chemoimmunotherapy (DRC or bendamustine-rituximab) or a continuous covalent BTK inhibitor, usually zanubrutinib. The choice rests on age, comorbidity, genotype, how fast IgM must fall and the patient's preference for a fixed or continuous treatment.
  • High IgM or hyperviscosity: plasmapheresis first; avoid rituximab alone because of the IgM flare8.
  • MYD88 wild-type disease: chemoimmunotherapy is often preferred; zanubrutinib is active31.
  • Relapse: the class not yet used, chemoimmunotherapy or a BTK inhibitor; a proteasome inhibitor regimen is an alternative.
  • After a covalent BTK inhibitor: venetoclax or pirtobrutinib, both off-label, or a clinical trial.

Open questions

  • Chemoimmunotherapy or a BTK inhibitor first? No randomized comparison has been reported.
  • Fixed-duration targeted treatment: can a BTK inhibitor with a BCL2 inhibitor replace continuous therapy? A trial of ibrutinib with venetoclax was stopped for ventricular arrhythmias in 9% of patients, two of them fatal, although very good partial responses reached 42%36; a time-limited combination of zanubrutinib, ixazomib and dexamethasone gave a median PFS of 40 months37. Newer combinations are in trials.
  • CXCR4-mutated and MYD88 wild-type disease: responses are slower or less deep, and no treatment is specific to them.
  • Resistance: what to give after both a covalent BTK inhibitor and venetoclax.
  • Complete response: rare with every current regimen; whether deeper responses change long-term outcome is not established.

What is coming

The trials under Trials to watch address the first two questions: ibrutinib-rituximab against DRC (RAINBOW), venetoclax-rituximab against DRC (VIWA-1) and against a BTK inhibitor (S2005), and carfilzomib added to ibrutinib (CZAR-1). After a BTK inhibitor, a phase 3 trial of the radiopharmaceutical iopofosine I 131 against rituximab, cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone is registered, and the BCL2 inhibitor sonrotoclax is in a phase 2 trial. BTK degraders, CAR T cells and an antibody-drug conjugate are under New drugs to watch.
