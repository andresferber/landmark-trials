Personal perspective, for education only. This text reflects the author's own reading and opinion of the published evidence. It is not a clinical practice guideline, medical advice or a substitute for clinical judgment. Patient care should follow the primary literature, current guidelines (such as NCCN, ESMO, IMWG and ASCO), approved prescribing information and the individual patient's circumstances.

Key points

  • Marginal zone lymphoma is four diseases with one name. Gastric MALT lymphoma, non-gastric MALT lymphoma, nodal and splenic marginal zone lymphoma differ in cause, presentation and first treatment, and they share trials only once systemic therapy is needed.
  • Where a driving infection exists, treating it comes first: H. pylori in the stomach, hepatitis C virus in splenic and other subtypes, and Chlamydophila psittaci in some ocular adnexal lymphomas.
  • Localized MALT lymphoma that persists is controlled by radiotherapy; 24 Gy is the standard dose, and response-adapted 4 Gy is under study for the stomach and the orbit.
  • For symptomatic splenic disease, rituximab alone has largely replaced splenectomy. Nodal disease has no trial of its own and is treated by analogy with follicular lymphoma.
  • The evidence for systemic therapy comes from one phase 3 trial in MALT lymphoma (IELSG-19) and from small marginal zone subgroups of indolent lymphoma trials, where benefit seen in follicular lymphoma was often not reproduced.
  • Three accelerated approvals for relapsed disease were granted on response rates; two (ibrutinib, umbralisib) were withdrawn. In the US, zanubrutinib, lenalidomide-rituximab and lisocabtagene maraleucel are the approved options at relapse.

Why the subtypes are kept apart, and where they meet

The four entities are separated on this page because their first decisions differ. A patient with gastric MALT lymphoma starts with antibiotics; one with a conjunctival lesion, with radiotherapy or doxycycline; one with splenic disease, with observation, rituximab or antiviral treatment; and one with nodal disease, with the reasoning used for follicular lymphoma. The trials of systemic treatment did not keep them apart. The older ones enrolled "MALT lymphoma" of any site, and the recent ones enrolled "marginal zone lymphoma" as a single cohort, usually as a minority beside follicular lymphoma. Each card therefore states which subtypes were enrolled, and how many patients of each when the paper says so.

Gastric MALT lymphoma: an infection first

Eradication of H. pylori gave complete histological remission in 80% of 120 patients with stage I1E disease followed for a median of 75 months, and 80% of those remissions were continuous1. A pooled analysis of 1408 patients found remission in 77.5%, and relapse in 7.2% of responders, about 2.2% per year2. In the largest single cohort, 77% of 420 patients responded and 10-year overall survival was 95%3.

Three findings shaped how failures are handled. First, t(11;18) marks tumors that will not regress: it was present in 42 of 63 non-responders and in only 2 of 48 responders, both of whom relapsed4. Second, persistent infiltrates are not failures if endoscopy is normal: 94% of 108 patients with minimal histological residuals had a favorable course without oncological treatment5, and the GELA grading gave pathologists a shared vocabulary for those biopsies6. Third, consolidation does not help: in the only randomized trial, chlorambucil after eradication did not reduce recurrence7. A trial of antibiotics is reasonable even without H. pylori; 5 of 6 such patients responded in the first report8. The European consensus brought these points together9.

When lymphoma persists or progresses, radiotherapy is the usual next step. With a median of 40 Gy, 10-year freedom from treatment failure was 88%10. A small randomized comparison found 25.2 Gy as effective as 36 Gy11, and a response-adapted pilot gave complete responses to 4 Gy in 20 of 24 patients, with 3-year local control of 96% after adding 20 Gy for the rest12. Rituximab is an alternative when radiotherapy does not suit, with responses in 77% of 26 patients13.

Non-gastric MALT lymphoma: local disease, distant relapse

The IELSG survey of 180 patients set the pattern: three quarters had stage I or II disease, yet 5-year PFS was 60% against an overall survival of 90%14. Radiotherapy controls the treated site almost always. In two large institutional series the relapses were mostly distant or in the paired organ, and risk depended on the organ of origin: 10-year recurrence-free rates were 95% for thyroid and 92% for stomach, but 68% for salivary gland and 67% for orbit15;16. A prospective trial of 24 to 30.6 Gy confirmed this, with 5-year PFS of 79% and two in-field failures among 68 patients17. Fields and doses were standardized by the ILROG18.

The dose question was put to a randomized test in indolent lymphoma as a whole: 4 Gy was inferior to 24 Gy, with 5-year local progression-free rates of 70.4% and 89.9%19. For the orbit, where 24 Gy causes cataract, a response-adapted approach starting with 4 Gy gave 2-year local control of 89.4% without grade 3 toxicity20. Doxycycline is an option for stage I ocular adnexal lymphoma, with responses in 65% and a 5-year PFS of 55%21.

Systemic treatment of MALT lymphoma

Rituximab alone gave responses in 73% of 35 patients, but they were short22. IELSG-19 remains the only phase 3 trial designed for this disease: rituximab with chlorambucil gave a 5-year EFS of 68%, against 51% with chlorambucil and 50% with rituximab alone, with 5-year overall survival of about 90% in every arm23. Because survival did not differ, a single agent is still a defensible choice for many patients. Bendamustine-rituximab, in a response-adapted schedule that needed more than four cycles in only 25% of patients, gave a 4-year EFS of 88%24; it has not been compared with rituximab-chlorambucil. Two years of subcutaneous rituximab maintenance after rituximab-chlorambucil raised complete remissions from 52% to 70%, in a single-arm trial that did not meet its primary endpoint25.

The IELSG-19 data set also produced the MALT-IPI, built on age, stage and LDH, with 5-year EFS of 70%, 56% and 29% across its groups26, and the proposal that complete response within 24 months can stand in for long-term PFS in future trials27.

Splenic marginal zone lymphoma

Diagnosis without splenectomy, criteria for starting treatment and response definitions come from a 2008 consensus28. Prognosis is described by two scores built on routine tests: the IIL score, with 5-year cause-specific survival from 88% to 50%29, and the HPLL score, with 5-year lymphoma-specific survival of 94%, 78% and 69%30.

The first treatment question is hepatitis C. In the original report all nine infected patients responded once the virus was cleared, and none of six uninfected patients responded to interferon31; with interferon-free antivirals the lymphoma responded in 73% of marginal zone lymphomas32. For other symptomatic patients, splenectomy corrected cytopenias in nearly all and gave a median PFS of 8.25 years33, but rituximab alone gave responses in 95% with a 5-year PFS of 73%, against 58% after splenectomy in a non-randomized comparison34. When chemoimmunotherapy is needed, bendamustine-rituximab gave complete responses in 73% and a 3-year PFS of 90%, at the price of grade 3 or higher neutropenia in 43%35.

NOTCH2 is the most frequently mutated gene, in about 20% to 25% of cases36, and a later classification separated two genetic clusters, with inferior survival in the one carrying NF-κB, NOTCH and KLF2 lesions37. Neither yet guides treatment.

Nodal marginal zone lymphoma

No prospective trial is specific to nodal disease. In the largest series, 5-year overall survival was 91%, a quarter of patients were still untreated at 5 years, and transformation reached 15% at 10 years38; an earlier series found a median PFS of 42.4 months and showed that FLIPI applies39. Sequencing found PTPRD lesions in 20%, absent from splenic disease40. In trials, nodal patients appear only inside mixed cohorts.

What the mixed trials show, and what they do not

For first systemic treatment, bendamustine-rituximab was better than R-CHOP in indolent lymphoma as a whole, but neither trial reports marginal zone lymphoma separately in its abstract41;42. Where the marginal zone subgroup was analyzed, the follicular lymphoma result was often not reproduced. Obinutuzumab did not improve PFS over rituximab and was more toxic, with fatal adverse events in 14.9% against 9.7%43. Rituximab maintenance, unlike in follicular lymphoma, delayed treatment failure compared with retreatment44. Lenalidomide-rituximab improved PFS in the whole AUGMENT population but not in its 63 patients with marginal zone lymphoma, although the regimen was approved for them45; as first treatment it gave responses in 93% of 30 patients46.

BTK inhibitors work in all three subtypes. Ibrutinib gave responses in 58%, with a median PFS of 15.7 months47; zanubrutinib in 68.2%, with a 24-month PFS of 70.9%48; acalabrutinib in 53%49; and pirtobrutinib in 55.6% of heavily pretreated patients, most of them after a covalent inhibitor50. These are single-arm trials of 36 to 68 patients. The confirmatory trial for ibrutinib added it to chemoimmunotherapy and did not improve PFS significantly51, and the indication was withdrawn. PI3K inhibitors followed a similar course: high response rates52;53, a positive randomized trial with rituximab54, and then withdrawal for safety.

CAR T cells give the deepest responses reported in relapsed disease: 77% in the 31 patients of ZUMA-5, which excluded splenic disease55, and 95% of 66 evaluable patients, of all three subtypes, with lisocabtagene maraleucel56.

Risk across subtypes

Progression within 24 months occurred in 18% of patients who needed immediate systemic therapy and carried a 3-year overall survival of 53%, against 95% without it57. The MZL-IPI, meant for any subtype at the start of systemic therapy, separates 5-year PFS of 85%, 66% and 37%58. Transformation was found in 3.8% of 340 patients, with no difference between subtypes59.

Where we are now

  • Gastric MALT lymphoma: eradication for every patient; observation of minimal residuals; radiotherapy for persistent or progressive localized disease; systemic treatment for the few with disseminated or symptomatic disease.
  • Non-gastric MALT lymphoma: radiotherapy for localized disease, at 24 Gy, with relapse mostly at distant sites; systemic treatment as below when needed.
  • Splenic: antiviral treatment if hepatitis C is present; observation without symptoms; rituximab alone for most symptomatic patients; bendamustine-rituximab or splenectomy for selected patients.
  • Nodal: observation or treatment along follicular lymphoma lines, without subtype-specific trials.
  • First systemic treatment: rituximab with chlorambucil or with bendamustine, or rituximab alone.
  • Relapse (US): zanubrutinib, lenalidomide-rituximab, and lisocabtagene maraleucel after two or more lines.

The list above agrees with the ESMO 2020 guideline, checked against its full text; the relapse options reflect current US labels, which postdate it (NCCN not checked).

Open questions

  • Whether response-adapted 4 Gy can replace 24 Gy outside single-center trials, for the stomach, the orbit and other sites.
  • Whether any treatment improves survival: no randomized trial in marginal zone lymphoma has shown it.
  • How chemoimmunotherapy, a BTK inhibitor and lenalidomide-rituximab compare, since none has been compared with another in this disease.
  • Whether results differ by subtype: the cohorts of each are too small to tell, and gastric and splenic patients are few or absent in several trials.
  • How to act on POD24, which is known only after the fact, and whether early complete response is a usable trial endpoint with drugs that rarely give complete responses.
  • Where CAR T cells and bispecific antibodies belong in a disease with long survival.

What is coming

The confirmatory trial for zanubrutinib compares it, with rituximab, against lenalidomide-rituximab (MAHOGANY). Two randomized trials test bispecific antibodies with lenalidomide at relapse (MARSUN with mosunetuzumab, OLYMPIA-5 with odronextamab), and the marginal zone cohort of the tafasitamab trial (InMIND) has not been reported. RITZ is the first randomized trial specific to splenic disease, comparing rituximab with and without zanubrutinib, and a phase 3 trial compares 24 Gy with a response-guided 4 Gy strategy. These are listed under Trials to watch.
