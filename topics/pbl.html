Key studies in primary bone lymphoma

A rare lymphoma with one small prospective trial and no randomized evidence. This page gathers what has been published: treatment series, registries, prognosis, single versus multiple lesions, biology, circulating tumor DNA, non-DLBCL histologies and imaging.

Key points

  • Primary bone lymphoma is rare, usually DLBCL of germinal-center type, and has a better outcome than nodal DLBCL of the same stage.
  • The evidence is one small prospective trial and many retrospective series. Nothing is randomized, and series define the disease differently.
  • Chemoimmunotherapy comes first. Radiotherapy alone is inferior, and giving radiotherapy before chemotherapy was associated with worse outcome.
  • Consolidative radiotherapy after chemoimmunotherapy remains the usual approach. The one multicenter rituximab-era study (Rezazadeh 2024) found no survival advantage from routine radiotherapy in limited-stage disease, with a possible role after a partial response. A single-center rituximab-era series (Abeyakoon 2025) found the opposite.
  • Age and IPI are the consistent prognostic factors. Fracture, soft-tissue extension and axial site are reported in some series and not others.
  • Whether multifocal bone-only disease is worse than unifocal disease is unsettled. It does better than stage IV DLBCL with bone involvement.
  • On circulating tumor DNA there is one case report. On non-DLBCL histologies there are three small series.

A rare disease with thin evidence

Primary bone lymphoma is lymphoma confined to one or more bones, with or without regional nodes, and without other disease. Most cases are DLBCL. Because it is rare, it has never had a randomized trial. The only prospective study closed early with 33 patients when rituximab arrived1. Everything else is retrospective, collected over decades in which staging, imaging and treatment all changed. Series also differ in what they call "primary": some accept only stage I–II, some include multifocal bone-only disease, and the largest recent cohort accepted limited disease outside bone2. The cards on this page state each definition, because results from differently defined cohorts cannot simply be set side by side.

Chemotherapy first

The older series agree on one point: radiotherapy alone is not enough. In Bologna, relapse followed radiotherapy alone in 4 of 7 complete responders against 2 of 35 after chemotherapy-based treatment3. At Memorial Sloan Kettering, 5-year overall survival was 95% with combined modality against 78% with a single modality4. In the IELSG-14 series, patients who started with chemotherapy did better than those who started with radiotherapy5, and among patients with a pathological fracture, irradiating the bone before chemotherapy was associated with a 5-year overall survival of 22% against 64% with the reverse order6. Rituximab improved outcome here as elsewhere: in British Columbia, 3-year progression-free survival was 88% with CHOP plus rituximab against 52% without it7.

Radiotherapy after chemoimmunotherapy: the usual approach

Consolidative radiotherapy after chemoimmunotherapy is the usual approach in limited-stage disease, and several lines of evidence support it. The early-stage Rare Cancer Network series reported 5-year local control of 92% with mostly combined treatment, and a better outcome with doses above 40 Gy8. In a single-center series in which most patients received rituximab, radiotherapy was associated with better 5-year progression-free survival (88% vs 63%) and overall survival (91% vs 68%), with no difference between 30–35 Gy and higher doses9. In the German trials of aggressive B-cell lymphoma, radiotherapy to sites of skeletal involvement was associated with better event-free survival (HR 0.3), although those patients were not limited to primary bone lymphoma10. The French series also suggested a role for consolidation11. In a rituximab-only cohort from Toronto, combined-modality treatment was associated with better 5-year progression-free survival (90% vs 70%) and overall survival (93% vs 79%), although half of the patients who had no radiotherapy had stage IV disease12.

The challenge to routine radiotherapy

The best rituximab-era data point the other way. In 112 patients with stage I–II primary bone DLBCL from 13 US centers, all treated with rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy, 10-year overall survival was 77.9% with consolidative radiotherapy and 89.0% without it (P=0.42), and relapse-free survival did not differ either13. Doses of 36 Gy or more were no better than lower doses. Of eight patients in partial response after chemoimmunotherapy, six were irradiated and five converted to complete response. The accompanying editorial, linked on that card, reads this as support for selective use: radiotherapy for a partial response, or to shorten chemotherapy when the field is low-risk, and no doses above 36 Gy.

The study has limits that matter. It is retrospective, the group without radiotherapy had only 34 patients, and that group received more chemotherapy (a mean of 5.6 cycles against 4.5). It therefore compares two strategies, a shorter course with radiotherapy and a longer course without, and it cannot exclude a modest benefit. Earlier data lean the same way without settling it: IELSG-14 found no advantage for consolidative radiotherapy after primary chemotherapy5, and an orthopedic series found no survival difference and more fractures and poorer function after radiotherapy14. In the largest recent cohort, radiotherapy was given mainly when complete response was not reached, and survival did not differ with or without it2. Until there is prospective evidence, radiotherapy after chemoimmunotherapy remains a reasonable standard, and the case for omitting it is strongest after a full course of chemoimmunotherapy and a complete metabolic response.

Prognosis

Outcome is good. Rituximab-era series report sustained complete remission in 84%11 and 3-year overall survival of 81.3%2. Registry survival is lower, with 5-year overall survival of 70.5% in adults with primary bone DLBCL15, as expected from an older and unselected population.

Age and IPI are the factors that recur. British Columbia separated three groups by age and IPI with 5-year overall survival of 90%, 61% and 25%7; age-adjusted IPI was the only independent factor in the French series11; and high-risk IPI doubled the hazard of death in the largest cohort (HR 2.06)2. Other factors are less consistent. Pathological fracture was independently adverse in IELSG-146 but had no effect in British Columbia. Soft-tissue extension was adverse at Moffitt16. Axial site was adverse in the registry17. CNS relapse was uncommon: 2.5% in limited-stage disease5.

One lesion or several

The studies that address this question answer different versions of it. Compared with stage IV DLBCL with skeletal involvement, multifocal bone-only DLBCL did clearly better (5-year overall survival 74% vs 36%)18. Within primary bone lymphoma, multifocal disease did worse than unifocal disease at Moffitt, but the effect was not independent of IPI, of which stage is a part16. Genomic work found unifocal and polyostotic DLBCL biologically alike19. PET complicates the picture further, because it shows more than one bone lesion in nearly half of patients2021. A fair summary is that multifocal bone-only disease sits between unifocal disease and ordinary stage IV DLBCL, and that no series shows how its treatment should differ.

Biology

Primary bone DLBCL is predominantly of germinal-center type, about 90% in the largest series22, with a centrocyte-like expression pattern, frequent mutations in B2M, EZH2, IRF8 and TNFRSF1423 and an EZB-like profile in a second cohort24. Cell of origin by immunohistochemistry did not predict survival in a small early series25. None of this guides treatment yet.

Circulating tumor DNA

The search found one publication: a case report in which persistent ctDNA after a complete metabolic remission led to CAR-T consolidation26. It shows that ctDNA can be followed in this disease. It says nothing about whether acting on it helps. Given how often bone lesions stay FDG-avid after remission, a blood-based measure of residual disease would be useful here, and this is an obvious gap.

Histologies other than DLBCL

Low-grade B-cell lymphoma presenting in bone is usually follicular and usually multifocal27. Most follicular lymphoma that presents in bone turns out to be systemic after staging28. An older IELSG report found poor long-term survival in indolent lymphoma of bone, but it mixed primary and secondary cases29. No adult series of Burkitt, lymphoblastic, T-cell or Hodgkin lymphoma of bone met the size threshold for this page.

Reading the PET

FDG PET is sensitive at staging. After treatment, a negative scan is reliable and a positive one often is not: in one small series the positive predictive value was 57%, with osteonecrosis among the false positives21. In a large DLBCL cohort with bone involvement, residual avidity at a bone site was seen in 9% of patients and was rarely true refractory disease30.

What is coming

No ongoing trial dedicated to primary bone lymphoma was found on ClinicalTrials.gov, and no new agent is being developed for this entity specifically, so the page has no "Trials to watch" or "New drugs to watch" list for now. Progress is more likely to come from trials of limited-stage DLBCL that report their bone subgroups, from PET-adapted and ctDNA-adapted strategies, and from larger collaborative retrospective cohorts.

What is not known

  • Whether consolidative radiotherapy adds anything after a complete metabolic response to rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy. There is no randomized trial, and the retrospective series disagree.
  • Whether PET can select patients who may omit radiotherapy. Bone lesions can stay FDG-avid after remission, and no bone-specific prospective data exist.
  • Whether multifocal bone-only disease is advanced-stage disease or the same entity as unifocal disease, and how it should be treated.
  • Whether CNS prophylaxis is ever justified. CNS relapse was 2.5% in limited-stage and 5.4% in multifocal disease in the IELSG-14 reports, with too few events to define a risk group.
  • Whether circulating tumor DNA has any role. One case report is all that has been published.
  • How to treat non-DLBCL histologies in bone. Only small series exist.
  • Whether newer first-line regimens for DLBCL change outcomes in this entity. No bone-specific data were found.
