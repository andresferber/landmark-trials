Key points

  • For fit patients, two decades of cytarabine-containing induction, autologous transplant and rituximab maintenance are giving way to BTK inhibitor-containing therapy, and routine transplant is no longer a given (TRIANGLE, E4151).
  • For older patients, bendamustine-rituximab replaced R-CHOP; adding a BTK inhibitor to it improves progression-free survival but not overall survival (SHINE, ECHO).
  • Chemotherapy-free frontline therapy is now supported by randomized data (ENRICH) and by high MRD-negative rates with BTK inhibitor and venetoclax combinations.
  • BTK inhibitors transformed relapsed disease; after BTK inhibitor failure, options now include pirtobrutinib, CD19 CAR-T, BCL2 inhibitors (venetoclax, sonrotoclax) and CD20×CD3 bispecifics.
  • TP53-altered disease remains the main unmet need at every stage.

Why mantle cell lymphoma is managed in two tracks

Mantle cell lymphoma ranges from indolent to highly aggressive disease, and most patients are older at diagnosis. Care has therefore developed along two tracks, intensive therapy for fit patients and gentler therapy for older or less fit patients, with a third track for relapse. Biology cuts across all three: Ki-67 emerged early as the main prognostic factor1, and TP53 alterations now define the group least served by any approach.

Fit patients: intensification, then de-escalation

The first randomized evidence that consolidation matters came from the European MCL Network, where autologous transplant in first remission more than doubled median PFS compared with interferon (39 vs 17 months)2. Two intensive strategies followed. In the US, R-HyperCVAD/MA without transplant produced complete responses in 87%, but with 8% treatment-related deaths3. In Europe, the Nordic group showed that cytarabine-containing induction followed by transplant gave long remissions, with a plateau in some patients1, and MCL Younger confirmed that alternating R-CHOP and R-DHAP before transplant more than doubled time to treatment failure (median 9.1 vs 3.9 years)4. LyMa then made rituximab maintenance after transplant standard, the first post-transplant step to improve survival (4-year overall survival 89% vs 80%)5.

BTK inhibitors have since reopened the question of how much intensity is needed. TRIANGLE added ibrutinib to induction and maintenance: 3-year failure-free survival was 88% with ibrutinib plus transplant and 86% with ibrutinib without transplant, against 72% with the standard transplant arm6. In E4151, patients in MRD-negative first remission had the same 3-year survival with or without transplant (82.1% vs 82.7%)7. WINDOW-1 showed that a BTK inhibitor-first approach can shorten the chemotherapy needed8.

Older patients: from R-CHOP to bendamustine and BTK inhibitors

MCL Elderly established R-CHOP over R-FC and showed that rituximab maintenance after R-CHOP improved survival (4-year overall survival 87% vs 63%)9. Bendamustine-rituximab then became the most widely used backbone: in StiL NHL1 the MCL subgroup had a median PFS of 35.4 vs 22.1 months with less toxicity than R-CHOP10, and BRIGHT confirmed it internationally while long-term data flagged more second cancers11. Bortezomib-based VR-CAP improved both PFS and overall survival over R-CHOP but saw limited uptake12.

Adding a BTK inhibitor to bendamustine-rituximab improved PFS in two phase 3 trials: ibrutinib in SHINE (median 80.6 vs 52.9 months), with more toxicity and no survival gain13, and acalabrutinib in ECHO (66.4 vs 49.6 months; survival HR 0.86, not significant), which led to approval in January 202514. ENRICH tested dropping chemotherapy altogether: ibrutinib-rituximab improved PFS overall, clearly against R-CHOP (HR 0.37) but not against bendamustine-rituximab (HR 0.91)15.

Chemotherapy-free and time-limited frontline therapy

Phase 2 studies have tested whether chemotherapy is needed at all. Lenalidomide-rituximab gave a 92% response rate and 85% 2-year PFS16. Ibrutinib, venetoclax and obinutuzumab produced complete metabolic responses in 86.6% of untreated patients, with a 5-year PFS of 80% reported in 202617, and an MRD-guided acalabrutinib-lenalidomide-anti-CD20 approach gave a 4-year PFS of 76%18. These approaches are especially relevant for TP53-altered disease, where chemotherapy works poorly.

Relapsed disease: the BTK inhibitor era and beyond

Before BTK inhibitors, relapse options were modest: bortezomib (response rate 33%, the first MCL-specific approval)19, temsirolimus20 and lenalidomide2122. Ibrutinib changed this, with a 68% response rate23 and more than double the PFS of temsirolimus in RAY (14.6 vs 6.2 months)24; its US MCL indication was later withdrawn (2023) when confirmatory requirements were not met. The more selective acalabrutinib (response rate 81%)25 and zanubrutinib (84%)26 followed.

After a covalent BTK inhibitor, prognosis was historically poor, and four strategies now address it. The noncovalent BTK inhibitor pirtobrutinib produced responses in 57.8%27. CD19 CAR-T gave complete responses in 67% with brexucabtagene autoleucel28 and 72.3% with lisocabtagene maraleucel29. BCL2 inhibition, first combined with ibrutinib in AIM30, improved PFS in SYMPATICO (31.9 vs 22.1 months)31, and sonrotoclax received accelerated approval in May 2026 after BTK inhibitors32. The CD20×CD3 bispecific glofitamab produced complete responses in 78.3%33, although it is not FDA-approved in MCL.

Where we are now

Published summaries of NCCN version 4.2026 describe the following; individual placements were not checked against the NCCN document itself.

  • Fit patients without TP53 alteration: the TRIANGLE ibrutinib-containing regimen is preferred, with acalabrutinib or zanubrutinib as possible substitutes. Patients in MRD-negative complete remission may omit transplant and proceed to rituximab maintenance with or without a BTK inhibitor.
  • Older or less fit patients: bendamustine-rituximab with or without acalabrutinib, or acalabrutinib-rituximab.
  • TP53-altered disease: clinical trials are strongly encouraged; a BTK inhibitor-venetoclax-obinutuzumab triplet is an option.
  • Relapse: a covalent BTK inhibitor if not used before; after BTK inhibitor failure, pirtobrutinib, CD19 CAR-T or a BCL2 inhibitor-based approach.

Open questions

  • Transplant: which fit patients still benefit, beyond those in MRD-negative remission?
  • Duration: can BTK inhibitor-based therapy be stopped, guided by MRD?
  • Survival: BTK inhibitor plus chemotherapy improves PFS without a survival gain so far; is chemotherapy-free therapy as good or better?
  • Sequencing after BTK inhibitors: CAR-T, bispecifics, pirtobrutinib and BCL2 inhibitors have not been compared head to head.
  • TP53: no approach yet gives durable control for most patients.

What is coming

Ongoing phase 3 trials (listed under Trials to watch at the end of this page) test zanubrutinib-rituximab against bendamustine-rituximab, continuous versus intermittent zanubrutinib, orelabrutinib with bendamustine-rituximab, pirtobrutinib against covalent BTK inhibitors, sonrotoclax with zanubrutinib, glofitamab against investigator's choice (GLOBRYTE) and rocbrutinib against BTK inhibitors.
