Richter transformation of CLL · first-line and relapsed · 2003–2026

Landmark studies in Richter transformation

Initial treatment and relapse in one topic, because no treatment is established at either point: risk, biology and diagnosis, chemoimmunotherapy and transplantation, BTK, BCL2 and checkpoint inhibitors, bispecific antibodies and CAR T cells, and the Hodgkin variant. There are no randomized trials yet; most entries are phase 2 trials and retrospective series, each with its design, verified results, context, open questions and reception.

PerspectiveRichter transformation: what the evidence supports, 2003–2026Read the perspective

Personal perspective, for education only. This text reflects the author's own reading and opinion of the published evidence. It is not a clinical practice guideline, medical advice or a substitute for clinical judgment. Patient care should follow the primary literature, current guidelines (such as NCCN, ESMO, IMWG and ASCO), approved prescribing information and the individual patient's circumstances.

Key points

  • Richter transformation develops in about 0.5% of patients with CLL per year. With the diffuse large B-cell type, median survival after transformation has stayed near one year across two decades of series.
  • Three questions set the prognosis: has the CLL been treated before, is TP53 disrupted, and is the lymphoma clonally related to the CLL. Untreated CLL and clonally unrelated lymphoma do much better.
  • There are no randomized trials. Chemoimmunotherapy produces responses in about half of patients and lasting remissions in few; allogeneic transplantation in remission is the best-documented route to long survival.
  • A BTK inhibitor alone gives responses in 40% to 50% that last months, enough to serve as a bridge. PD-1 inhibitors work poorly alone and better with a BTK or BCL2 inhibitor.
  • CAR T cells and CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies give complete responses in a minority of patients, and those complete responses can last for years. Median PFS is still 3 to 5 months.
  • Relapsed disease has no separate standard: the same options are used in a different order, and results are worse. A clinical trial is the first choice at every step.
  • The Hodgkin variant is a different problem: it is treated as classical Hodgkin lymphoma, with 2-year survival around 70%.

Why first-line and relapsed disease are together

In most lymphomas the evidence divides naturally into first treatment and relapse. In Richter transformation it does not. No regimen has been established by a randomized trial at either point, most trials enroll untreated and previously treated patients together, and what is used at relapse is whatever was not used first. Results at relapse are poor with every approach: in the KEYNOTE-170 cohort, where 83% of patients had received two or more lines of therapy, median survival was 3.8 months1. This Perspective therefore follows the disease from diagnosis onward and notes, for each treatment, what is known in untreated and in relapsed patients.

How often, and in whom

In newly diagnosed CLL followed at one center, transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma occurred in 2.3% of patients, about 0.5% per year, and 1% per year once CLL had been treated2. Among 2,975 patients in the German first-line trials, 3% developed Richter transformation, and median survival afterwards was 9 months3. The risk follows the biology of the CLL (unmutated IGHV, high-risk FISH abnormalities, stereotyped B-cell receptors) and its treatment: a purine analogue with an alkylating agent tripled it2.

Genomic studies have changed the picture of where the lymphoma comes from. Clonally related Richter transformation arises from the CLL clone by acquiring about 20 additional lesions, with loss of TP53 or CDKN2A in about half of cases, and its genome is distinct from that of de novo diffuse large B-cell lymphoma45. Subclones with the features of Richter cells can be found at the diagnosis of CLL, up to 19 years before transformation, and they show low B-cell receptor signaling6. A later study of 52 patients defined molecular subtypes and suggested that cell-free DNA might detect transformation early7. None of this guides treatment yet, but it helps explain why drugs that work in CLL work poorly here.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis needs tissue. PET selects the site: in 332 patients, an SUVmax of 10 or more was associated with a median survival of 6.9 months against 56.7 months below 10, and fine-needle aspiration was inadequate in 53% of cases of Richter syndrome8. That threshold came from patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy. After ibrutinib or idelalisib, an SUVmax of 10 had a sensitivity of 71% and a specificity of 50%, and 5 of 6 patients who later transformed had started below it9. The ERIC consensus asks for PET-CT, an excisional biopsy of the most FDG-avid accessible lesion, and testing of the clonal relationship to the CLL10.

Prognosis: three questions

The first prognostic score used five clinical factors: performance status, LDH, platelets, tumor size and number of prior therapies11. Later work reduced the problem to three questions.

Is the lymphoma clonally related to the CLL? Unrelated cases had a median survival of 62.5 months against 14.2 months, and less TP53 disruption12. The EHA guidelines treat clonally unrelated disease as de novo diffuse large B-cell lymphoma13.

Is TP53 disrupted? It was in 47.1% of cases, and its absence was associated with a 57% lower risk of death12.

Has the CLL been treated? In 204 patients, median survival was 46.3 months when CLL had never been treated and 7.8 months when it had14. The Spanish series combined prior therapy, TP53 alterations and a low platelet count: median survival was 75.3 months with none of these factors and 3 months with two or more15. The same holds after targeted therapy: among 309 patients who never received chemoimmunotherapy, median survival was 12.3 months when CLL had been treated, mostly with BTK or BCL2 inhibitors, against 59.6 months when it had not16.

These three answers matter more than the choice among current regimens, and they should be known before comparing the results of single-arm trials, which differ widely in the proportion of untreated patients.

Chemoimmunotherapy

R-CHOP is the default by analogy with de novo lymphoma. In a prospective phase 2 trial, 15 patients with Richter transformation had a response rate of 67%, with a median PFS of 10 months and a median survival of 21 months17. Replacing rituximab with ofatumumab and adding maintenance gave a response rate of 46%, a median PFS of 6.2 months and a median survival of 11.4 months18. More intensive regimens did not help. Hyper-CVXD with rituximab had an early death rate of 18% in the first cycle19; the platinum-based OFAR regimen gave responses in 50% and, in its second study, a median survival of 6.6 months20; and first-line R-EPOCH gave a median PFS of 3.5 months, with 30% of patients dying without progression or response21.

Adding venetoclax to dose-adjusted R-EPOCH produced complete responses in 13 of 26 patients, a median PFS of 10.1 months and a median survival of 19.6 months, with grade 3 or higher neutropenia in 65%22. In routine practice, venetoclax with R-CHOP gave complete responses in 46% of 13 patients, and venetoclax with a BTK inhibitor in 25% of 2823. Among the chemotherapy-based treatments on this page these are the highest complete response rates, in small and selected groups.

Transplantation

The observation that patients transplanted in remission live longer goes back to 20 patients in the first large series11. In the EBMT registry, 3-year survival was 36% after allogeneic and 59% after autologous transplantation, the latter mostly in chemosensitive disease24. In the CIBMTR registry, 3-year survival after allogeneic transplantation was 52%, and the depth of response decided the outcome: 77% in complete response, 57% in partial response and 15% with resistant disease, while del(17p) and prior targeted therapy made no difference25. With more than 6 years of follow-up, 3-year survival after allogeneic transplantation was 39% and nonrelapse mortality 38%26.

All of these series count only patients who reached transplantation. With that limit, allogeneic transplantation in remission remains the best-documented way to long survival in clonally related disease, and the EHA guidelines advise considering it in eligible patients who respond13.

BTK inhibitors

Acalabrutinib alone gave responses in 40.0% of 25 patients, with a median duration of 6.2 months and a median PFS of 3.2 months27. Pirtobrutinib gave responses in 50.0% of 82 patients, 90% of whom had already been treated for Richter transformation and 74% with a covalent BTK inhibitor; eight responders went on to transplantation. Responses lasted a median of 7.4 months, median PFS was 3.7 months and median survival 12.5 months28. A small cohort treated with zanubrutinib, few of them previously exposed to a BTK inhibitor, had longer remissions29. The reasonable reading is that a BTK inhibitor is a bridge and a partner in combinations, and not a treatment by itself, which is how the EHA guidelines place acalabrutinib and pirtobrutinib13.

Checkpoint inhibitors

Pembrolizumab produced responses in 4 of 9 patients with Richter transformation and in none of 16 with CLL30. The confirmatory cohort was negative: 3 responses in 23 patients, two of them in the Hodgkin variant, and a median PFS of 1.6 months1. Combinations did better. Nivolumab with ibrutinib gave responses in 13 of 20 patients in one trial and in 10 of 24 in another, where responses lasted a median of 15 months3132. In RT1, tislelizumab with zanubrutinib gave a response rate of 58.3%, a median PFS of 10.0 months and a 12-month survival of 74.7%; only 20.8% of the patients had been treated for Richter transformation before33. In MOLTO, a first-line combination of atezolizumab, venetoclax and obinutuzumab gave responses in 67.9% of 28 patients and complete responses in 28.6%, without tumor lysis syndrome; at 12 months, PFS was 42.9% and survival 64.3%34.

Two smaller trials are a caution. A fixed-duration triplet of obinutuzumab, ibrutinib and venetoclax, given to 12 patients with a median age of 78, produced early metabolic responses that did not last: median PFS was 4.4 months and median survival 7.8 months35. In relapsed disease, copanlisib with nivolumab gave responses in 4 of 13 patients, with a median PFS of 2.0 months36.

The better chemotherapy-free regimens have response rates similar to R-CHOP with less toxicity, mostly as first treatment. They have not been compared with chemoimmunotherapy, follow-up is short, and the results are not uniform.

Bispecific antibodies and CAR T cells

The treatments that redirect T cells share a pattern: fewer than half of patients reach complete response, but those who do can stay in remission for years. In the international CAR T-cell series, 46% of 69 heavily pretreated patients reached complete response, and those responses lasted a median of 27.6 months, while median PFS for the whole group was 4.7 months and median survival 8.5 months; severe neurotoxicity occurred in 37%37. Epcoritamab gave responses in 47.6% of 42 patients, 57.1% in first line and 38.1% later, and complete responses in 40%; median PFS was 3.0 months overall, and 8.5 months in first line against 2.9 months later38. Mosunetuzumab in relapsed disease gave complete responses in 20% of 20 patients, with a median PFS of 3.4 months39. With glofitamab, 5 of 11 refractory patients reached complete response, and 4 of them were still in remission at a median follow-up of 67.1 months after a fixed course, without transplantation40. Blinatumomab was tested alone in 9 patients and then after two cycles of R-CHOP, where it converted 20% of patients not in complete response4142.

How these treatments relate to transplantation is not settled. In the series of patients without prior chemoimmunotherapy, median survival was 54.5 months after allogeneic transplantation and 9.0 months after CAR T cells, but the two groups were not comparable16.

The Hodgkin variant

Transformation to classical Hodgkin lymphoma is about one tenth as frequent, 0.05% per year, with a median survival of 3.9 years in the Mayo Clinic cohort43. In 94 patients from a multicenter series, 2-year survival was 72%, patients given ABVD-based treatment had a median survival of 13.2 years, and transplantation in first remission added nothing44. Population-based data were less favorable overall, with a 2-year survival of 57%, but 74% in patients able to receive curative-intent ABVD45. In the Spanish series, median survival was 30.8 months for the Hodgkin type against 5.9 months for the diffuse large B-cell type15. It is treated as classical Hodgkin lymphoma13.

Where we are now

No drug is approved for Richter transformation, and no standard exists. The EHA guidelines give three graded recommendations for the diffuse large B-cell type: test the clonal relationship, offer a clinical trial whenever possible, and outside trials use chemoimmunotherapy, the last with the lowest grade of recommendation13. The ERIC consensus says the same about trials10. NCCN placements were not checked.

In practice the sequence is built from the pieces above. Clonally unrelated lymphoma, and to a large extent transformation in never-treated CLL, is treated as de novo diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. For clonally related disease after CLL therapy, a first treatment is chosen to obtain a response (a trial, R-CHOP with or without venetoclax, or a chemotherapy-free combination), and responders who are fit are considered for allogeneic transplantation. At relapse, the options are a noncovalent BTK inhibitor as a bridge, CAR T cells or a bispecific antibody, again preferably in a trial. This is an interpretation of single-arm data, and reasonable clinicians order these steps differently.

Open questions

  • Chemotherapy or not: do checkpoint, BTK and BCL2 inhibitor combinations match R-CHOP as first treatment? No comparison exists3334.
  • Consolidation: after a complete response to a bispecific antibody or CAR T cells, is allogeneic transplantation still needed?
  • After targeted therapy: most patients now transform during or after a BTK or BCL2 inhibitor, and their median survival is still about one year16.
  • Diagnosis: the PET threshold after targeted therapy, and whether cell-free DNA can replace or anticipate biopsy97.
  • End points: response rates in single-arm trials of 20 to 80 patients have not translated into survival gains; the ERIC consensus asks that trials prioritize survival10.

What is coming

The first randomized trials are under way (listed under Trials to watch): pirtobrutinib added to R-CHOP in a phase 3 trial (PIRAMID), pirtobrutinib with epcoritamab against R-CHOP (CLLRT2), and CHOP-R with or without acalabrutinib (STELLAR). Single-arm trials are adding glofitamab or mosunetuzumab to CHOP as first treatment (GLORIFY, GELLC9RICHTER) and sonrotoclax to tislelizumab and zanubrutinib (CLL-RT1). Early data on a BTK degrader and on epcoritamab combinations are under New drugs to watch.
