Landmark studies in AL amyloidosis

It is not a clinical practice guideline, medical advice or a substitute for clinical judgment. Patient care should follow the primary literature, current guidelines (such as NCCN, ESMO, IMWG and ASCO), approved prescribing information and the individual patient's circumstances.

Key points

  • A monoclonal protein in a patient with amyloid does not make the amyloid AL. The deposit itself must be typed before chemotherapy, by mass spectrometry where possible.
  • The hematologic screen is serum free light chains with immunofixation of both serum and urine. Bone scintigraphy is positive in about half of patients with cardiac AL, so a positive scan with a monoclonal protein needs a biopsy with typing.
  • The heart decides early survival. Mayo 2004 staging with the European stage IIIb is the working standard; the AL International Staging System adds an ultra-high-risk stage IIIC.
  • The aim of treatment is a hematologic complete response, reached quickly, because organ recovery and survival follow the depth of response.
  • Daratumumab with bortezomib, cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone is the standard first treatment, with longer overall survival in the final ANDROMEDA analysis. Stage IIIb, excluded from that trial, remains the main unmet need.
  • Fibril-directed antibodies have not improved outcomes overall in randomized trials; a signal in κ disease with anselamimab needs confirmation.

Proving that the amyloid is AL

For years, a monoclonal protein in a patient with systemic amyloid was taken as proof of AL type. Genetic screening of 350 patients diagnosed with AL amyloidosis without a family history found an amyloidogenic mutation in 9.7%, most often in fibrinogen A α-chain or transthyretin, and a quarter of those patients also had a low-grade monoclonal gammopathy1. The overlap is larger in older patients: a monoclonal gammopathy was found in 39% of patients with wild-type ATTR and 49% with ATTR V122I2. Clinical features help but do not type the amyloid; AL, hereditary ATTR and wild-type ATTR behave as different cardiac diseases3.

The deposit itself has to be typed. Abdominal fat aspiration is the simplest tissue test, with a sensitivity of 80% on routine reading and 93% with careful reading of three smears4. Immunohistochemistry read by an expert pathologist typed most cases5, but laser microdissection with mass spectrometry, introduced in 2009 with 98% accuracy in a cardiac validation set6, is now the reference method. In 16,175 specimens typed by mass spectrometry, AL accounted for 59.0% and ATTR for 28.4%, followed by ALECT2, AA and heavy chain amyloid, with an organ-by-type map that sets the pretest probability of AL for each biopsy site7. In the kidney, about one amyloid in seven was not immunoglobulin-derived, and immunofluorescence missed 7.3% of light chain cases8.

The clone is found with blood and urine tests, and no single test is enough. Immunofixation was more sensitive than the free light chain ratio (96% against 76%), and serum and urine immunofixation together with the free light chain ratio identified the amyloidogenic light chain in every patient9.

Cardiac imaging and the boundary with ATTR

Cardiac MRI showed the typical global subendocardial late enhancement of cardiac amyloid10, but it does not separate AL from ATTR. Bone scintigraphy does so only in part. Myocardial uptake was more than 99% sensitive for cardiac ATTR, and grade 2 or 3 uptake with no monoclonal protein in serum or urine was 100% specific, allowing ATTR to be diagnosed without a biopsy; the false positives came almost entirely from cardiac AL11. Half of patients with cardiac AL took up the tracer, usually at low grade12. The European cardiology algorithm therefore starts with scintigraphy and the monoclonal protein tests together, and requires tissue typing whenever a monoclonal protein is found13. For hematologists, this means that the patient with a monoclonal gammopathy and a positive scan needs a biopsy, often endomyocardial, before any treatment.

Staging: the heart decides

The Mayo 2004 system used NT-proBNP and troponin to define three stages, with median survival of 26.4, 10.5 and 3.5 months14. Stage III proved too broad: among 346 patients in stage III, NT-proBNP above 8500 ng/L and systolic pressure below 100 mm Hg identified a group with a median survival of 3 months, and 42% of all stage III patients died before their first response assessment15. This European stage IIIb has since defined trial eligibility. The revised Mayo 2012 system added the size of the clone (dFLC)16, and a BNP-based version serves centres without NT-proBNP17. The kidney has its own stages, built on proteinuria and eGFR18.

The clone also carries prognosis. More than 10% marrow plasma cells carried an outlook similar to overt myeloma19, and t(11;14), the commonest cytogenetic abnormality, was associated with shorter survival with bortezomib-dexamethasone, while the myeloma high-risk abnormalities were not20. In the daratumumab era, two newer tools refine cardiac risk. The AL International Staging System adds echocardiographic strain and defines a stage IIIC, about 5% of patients, with a median survival of 7 months21. Extracellular volume on cardiac MRI measures the amyloid load and suggests that the depth of response needed for survival rises with it22.

Response: how deep and how fast

The 2005 consensus gave the field a common language for organ involvement23. The 2012 criteria, validated against survival in 816 patients, defined complete response, very good partial response (dFLC below 40 mg/L) and partial response, and showed that free light chain and NT-proBNP changes predict survival as early as 3 months24. They made early response the reason to change treatment. Cardiac response is now graded: 5-year overall survival was 93%, 79%, 65% and 33% for cardiac complete, very good partial, partial and no response25. Even complete hematologic response may leave minimal residual disease, found in 54% of complete responders and associated with fewer organ responses26.

First-line treatment: from melphalan to daratumumab

Oral melphalan with high-dose dexamethasone gave hematologic responses in 67% of patients ineligible for transplantation27. High-dose melphalan with transplantation gave a complete response in 40% and a median survival of 4.6 years in selected patients, at the cost of 13% mortality within 100 days28. The only randomized comparison found no advantage for transplantation: median survival was 22.2 months against 56.9 months with melphalan-dexamethasone, and transplant-related mortality was 24%29. Transplantation has since become safer, with 100-day mortality falling from 20% to 5% in North America30, and a small randomized trial suggested that bortezomib induction before it deepens response31. Whether transplantation adds to modern induction is being tested again.

Bortezomib changed the backbone. CyBorD gave a hematologic response in 60% of 230 patients, but only 42% in stage IIIb32. Adding bortezomib to melphalan-dexamethasone raised the 3-month response rate from 52% to 79% and halved mortality, the first survival gain in a controlled trial33. ANDROMEDA then added subcutaneous daratumumab to VCd: hematologic complete response rose from 18.1% to 53.3%, and cardiac and renal responses roughly doubled34. At a median follow-up of 61.4 months, complete response was 59.5% against 19.2%, and both major organ deterioration progression-free survival (HR 0.44) and overall survival (HR 0.62) were longer. The FDA granted accelerated approval in January 2021 and traditional approval in November 2025.

Stage IIIb

Patients in stage IIIb were excluded from ANDROMEDA and from most trials. In the EMN22 trial, daratumumab alone in 40 such patients gave a hematologic response within a median of 1 week and a 6-month overall survival of 65%, with cardiac failure and sudden death as the main serious events35. The US prescribing information does not recommend daratumumab for stage IIIb outside trials, while the EHA-ISA guideline lists daratumumab, alone or in dose-modified combinations, for these patients.

Relapsed disease

Treatment at relapse borrows from myeloma and is chosen by previous exposure, depth and duration of the first response, and frailty. Lenalidomide at a reduced dose36, pomalidomide37, once-weekly bortezomib38 and bendamustine, used mainly when the clone is lymphoplasmacytic39, all came from single-arm trials. The only phase 3 trial at relapse, TOURMALINE-AL1, did not improve the hematologic response rate with ixazomib (53% against 51%), although time to vital organ deterioration or death was longer40. Anti-CD38 antibodies gave fast, deep responses in daratumumab-naive patients: very good partial response or better in 47.5%41 and complete or very good partial response in 86%42, and 57% with isatuximab43. In patients with t(11;14), venetoclax-based regimens gave very good partial or complete responses in 78%, against 30% without the translocation, in a retrospective series44.

Removing the amyloid

Chemotherapy stops new light chain production but does not remove what is deposited. NEOD001, later birtamimab, was well tolerated and associated with cardiac and renal responses in a first trial45, but the phase 3 VITAL trial was stopped for futility; a post hoc survival benefit in Mayo stage IV disease was not confirmed by AFFIRM-AL, as reported by the sponsor46. In the two CARES trials of anselamimab with chemotherapy in stage IIIa and IIIb disease, the primary end point was not met; in 72 patients with κ disease, mortality and cardiovascular hospitalizations were lower, a hypothesis that needs confirmation47. Doxycycline added to CyBorD did not improve progression-free survival in a randomized trial48.

Where we are now

Typing the deposit, ideally by mass spectrometry, and testing for the clone with free light chains and serum and urine immunofixation are the start of every case; a positive bone scan does not exclude AL in a patient with a monoclonal protein. Patients are staged by the heart, with the European IIIb and, increasingly, the AL-ISS IIIC. For most newly diagnosed patients, daratumumab-VCd is the standard; transplantation is reserved for selected patients. The goal is a hematologic complete response, ideally with a very low involved free light chain, and treatment is changed early when the response is inadequate (EHA-ISA 2023; NCCN not checked). At relapse, the choice follows previous exposure, with venetoclax for t(11;14). No fibril-directed therapy is approved.

Open questions

  • How to treat stage IIIb and IIIC disease, where many deaths occur before any treatment can work.
  • Whether transplantation adds anything after daratumumab-based induction, and how long daratumumab should be continued.
  • Whether treatment should be guided by cardiac amyloid load, MRD, or t(11;14) status, none of which has been tested prospectively.
  • Whether the κ subgroup benefit with anselamimab is real.
  • Which treatment after daratumumab: no randomized trial has addressed this setting.

What is coming

BCMA-directed therapies are moving into first-line trials. Three phase 3 trials, not yet recruiting, compare linvoseltamab, etentamig and NXC-201 CAR T cells with daratumumab-CyBorD in newly diagnosed patients, and SWOG S2213 tests transplantation after daratumumab-VCd induction. These trials and the newer agents are on the Trials to watch and New drugs to watch tabs.
